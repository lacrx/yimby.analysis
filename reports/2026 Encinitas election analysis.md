# Encinitas voters narrowly reverse the bikelash wave

The November 2026 Encinitas election is producing a razor-thin correction to the anti-urbanist wave that swept the city in 2024. With ballots still being counted, challenger **Rimga Viskanta leads incumbent Mayor Bruce Ehlers by just 67 votes** (50.18% to 49.82%), **Arielle Golden leads Ed Sprague by 109 votes** in District 3 (50.96% to 49.04%), and **Brad Lefkowits holds a commanding 856-vote lead** over appointed incumbent Marco San Antonio in District 4 (57.24% to 42.76%) ([NC Pipeline](https://ncpipeline.substack.com/p/2026-election-results-tight-races); [iNewsource](https://inewsource.org/group-chat/friday-encinitas-election-results-mayor-city-council/)). If these leads hold through certification on December 3, the council flips from a **4-1 anti-mandate majority to a 3-2 moderate majority** in just two years. But the winning candidates are not running on YIMBY or urbanist platforms. Neither YIMBY Action nor California YIMBY endorsed in any Encinitas race ([YIMBY Action](https://yimbyaction.org/endorsements/november-2026-california-general)). The bikelash faction overreached on tone and confrontation; the correction is toward pragmatic moderation, not toward density or protected bike lanes. Every candidate at the 2026 forum supported two parking spots per unit and opposed CEQA waivers for housing ([iNewsource](https://inewsource.org/group-chat/encinitas-election-politics-housing/)). The Overton window in Encinitas has not moved toward urbanism. It has moved away from combat.

## Two infrastructure projects built the backlash; 2024 delivered the wave

The bikelash narrative in Encinitas has two origin points, not one. The **Leucadia Streetscape** on Highway 101, a 2.2-mile corridor project adding roundabouts, lane reductions, bike lanes, and sidewalks, was the slow-burn grievance. Discussed as early as 1995 and approved in March 2018, the project generated years of construction disruption, with businesses complaining it drove away customers, and critics arguing the city spent **over $20 million on surface upgrades while underground drainage in Leucadia remained underfunded** ([San Diego Reader](https://sandiegoreader.com/news/2018/mar/30/encinitas-roundabout-capital-california); [NBC San Diego](https://nbcsandiego.com/news/local/leucadia-businesses-say-highway-101-construction-is-driving-customers-away/4059400); [Voice of San Diego](https://voiceofsandiego.org/2026/01/08/encinitas-still-blames-former-mayor-catherine-blakespear-for-many-of-its-troubles/)). By April 2020, more than **100 residents protested outside then-Mayor Catherine Blakespear's home** over COVID-era closures and infrastructure frustrations, including the Coastal Rail Trail ([Voice of San Diego](https://voiceofsandiego.org/2026/01/08/encinitas-still-blames-former-mayor-catherine-blakespear-for-many-of-its-troubles/)).

The **Santa Fe Drive Corridor** was the acute flashpoint. Funded by **$3.09 million in state money** that Sen. Blakespear secured in July 2023, the $4.15 million project installed Class IV protected bike lanes, reverse-angle parking, and lane narrowing. The implementation was botched: a combined bike lane/sidewalk left inches between the sidewalk and the travel lane in places; the **U.S. Post Office stopped delivering mail for months**; the city fired the contractor in December 2024 over shoddy work; and residents cited fire/ambulance access problems and seven blind right-hook turns ([NC Pipeline](https://ncpipeline.substack.com/p/blakespears-bike-bill-ignites-encinitas-feud); [CBS 8](https://www.cbs8.com/article/news/local/encinitas-to-scale-back-4m-bike-lane-project-after-complaints/509-40996632-f4cf-4532-a631-56c90829ded4)). One commission member presented data showing a **more than 400% increase in serious crashes** compared to the 14 years before installation ([CBS 8](https://www.cbs8.com/article/news/local/encinitas-to-scale-back-4m-bike-lane-project-after-complaints/509-40996632-f4cf-4532-a631-56c90829ded4)). The state funding created a direct link between Blakespear's Sacramento role and the local controversy, making it personal and intergovernmental.

The initial research premise that the bikelash wave hit in 2022 is wrong. In **2022**, Blakespear departed for state Senate, Tony Kranz won the mayoral race with **48.18% in a four-candidate field**, and Ehlers won the District 4 council seat; Joy Lyndes won District 3. The council remained progressive-leaning ([North Coast Current](https://www.northcoastcurrent.com/encinitas/2022/11/kranz-holds-lead-for-encinitas-mayor-district-3-close-cannabis-tax-winning/); [Coast News](https://thecoastnews.com/kranz-to-be-next-mayor-encinitas-ehlers-wins-district-4-seat/)). The real wave came in **2024**: Ehlers beat Kranz **52.4% to 47.6%**, Luke Shaffer won District 1 by 13 points (56.4%), and Jim O'Hara won District 2 by 11 points (55.7%), creating a **4-1 anti-mandate supermajority** with Joy Lyndes as the lone progressive voice ([Patch](https://patch.com/california/encinitas/encinitas-mayor-race-flips-ehlers-leads-slim-margin); [North Coast Current](https://www.northcoastcurrent.com/encinitas/2024/11/encinitas-appears-headed-toward-city-council-sea-change/)). Tony Kranz later said Ehlers' promise to "stop development" was the main reason he lost ([iNewsource](https://inewsource.org/2026/10/07/encinitas-california-city-council-mayor-2026-election/)).

## The Ehlers majority fought the state and the state won

Once in power, the Ehlers council pursued a multi-front anti-mandate agenda that produced confrontation but few lasting policy victories. In **February 2026**, the council voted to remove the protected bike lane in front of San Dieguito Academy High School. In **March**, they voted **4-1** (Lyndes dissenting) to redesign the entire Santa Fe corridor from Class IV protected lanes to Class II painted lanes, replace reverse-angle parking with parallel spots, and widen lanes to 11 feet, at an estimated cost of **$3.4 to $3.6 million** to redo a project that cost $4.15 million to build ([KPBS](https://www.kpbs.org/news/politics/2026/09/10/the-encinitas-city-council-wants-to-remove-a-protected-bike-lane-a-state-senator-is-trying-to-save-it)). Ehlers argued that "protected bike lanes are not necessarily safer than painted lanes" and that buffered lanes let cars pull over for emergency vehicles ([KPBS](https://www.kpbs.org/news/politics/2026/10/12/meet-the-candidates-running-for-encinitas-mayor-2026-general-election)).

Sen. Blakespear responded with SB 569, a statewide bike infrastructure protection bill that died in the Assembly. She then secured **budget trailer bill language specifically barring Encinitas from removing or modifying the Santa Fe Drive protected lane for five years**. The council voted 4-1 in September 2026 to ask Governor Newsom to line-item veto the provision. Ehlers and Deputy Mayor O'Hara called it a "personal vendetta" ([Streetsblog SD](https://sd.streetsblog.org/2026/09/08/california-legislature-moves-to-stop-encinitas-from-tearing-out-state-funded-santa-fe-bike-lanes/); [NC Pipeline](https://ncpipeline.substack.com/p/blakespears-bike-bill-ignites-encinitas-feud)). The state-vs-city confrontation put the bike lane dispute in the news throughout the campaign season.

On housing, the council endorsed the **Our Neighborhood Voices** ballot initiative, which would amend the California constitution to let local governments override conflicting state housing laws. The initiative failed to qualify for the 2022, 2024, or 2026 ballots; the June 25, 2026 deadline passed without qualification ([Manifold Markets](https://manifold.markets/DavidFWatson/will-our-neighborhood-voices-qualif)). State lawmakers dismissed the effort. Blakespear said she sees **"no appetite in Sacramento"** for the changes Encinitas wants, and Assemblymember Chris Ward warned courts would uphold state law ([Voice of San Diego](https://voiceofsandiego.org/2025/03/07/encinitas-is-banking-on-the-state-to-let-them-limit-housing-but-lawmakers-arent-interested/)).

When confronted with direct enforcement, the council complied. In **February 2025**, they voted **3-1** to approve the **448-unit Quail Meadows project** (90 affordable units) after HCD warned that rejection could trigger Housing Element decertification and Builder's Remedy exposure. Ehlers called it a **"Sophie's choice"** ([Voice of San Diego](https://voiceofsandiego.org/2025/12/29/north-county-cities-are-conforming-to-state-housing-laws-but-working-to-change-them-behind-the-scenes/)). The progressive civic group Encinitas Action claims the current council has approved every housing development that has come before it, despite the anti-mandate rhetoric ([Encinitas Action](https://encinitasaction.org/encinitas-housing-timeline-how-we-got-here-and-what-residents-should-know/)). The pattern is consistent with prior Encinitas councils: rhetorical opposition to state mandates followed by compliance when penalties loom. The difference under Ehlers was the volume and visibility of the rhetoric, not the policy outcomes.

## All six candidates reject density; the divide is over confrontation versus cooperation

The 2026 races pit three Ehlers-aligned candidates (Ehlers, Ed Sprague, Marco San Antonio) against three Democratic-endorsed challengers (Viskanta, Golden, Lefkowits), but the policy distance between the slates is smaller than the factional warfare suggests.

**Bruce Ehlers** is the most explicitly anti-density figure in the race: author of Proposition A (2013), which requires voter approval for any zoning density or height increase; fired from the Planning Commission in April 2022 for opposing the city's housing plans; and running on a platform to **"defend against overdevelopment"** and restore local control over zoning ([KPBS](https://www.kpbs.org/news/politics/2026/10/12/meet-the-candidates-running-for-encinitas-mayor-2026-general-election)). He is the only candidate who opposes even ADUs as infill housing ([iNewsource](https://inewsource.org/group-chat/encinitas-election-politics-housing/)). **Rimga Viskanta**, a San Dieguito Union High School District trustee and registered Democrat, takes a moderate reform position. She says the city **"cannot count on large-scale private developers to solve the affordable housing crisis,"** favors partnering with nonprofit developers, protecting Encinitas's 11 mobile home parks through rent-increase limits, and strengthening the city's 20% inclusionary zoning ordinance. Crucially, she disagrees with state housing policy but opposes the "fight the state" litigation strategy, preferring to **"work collaboratively with legislators"** ([KPBS](https://www.kpbs.org/news/politics/2026/10/12/meet-the-candidates-running-for-encinitas-mayor-2026-general-election); [Voice of San Diego](https://voiceofsandiego.org/2026/09/23/two-opposite-cities-one-similar-battle-for-mayor/)). On transportation, Viskanta supported the staff-recommended changes to Santa Fe Drive (keeping protected infrastructure) that the council rejected, and she frames safer streets around **child safety and "tomorrow's mobility choices"** rather than urbanist ideology. She explicitly criticized the council for **"designing traffic projects from the dais"** instead of deferring to staff expertise ([Voice of San Diego](https://voiceofsandiego.org/2026/09/23/two-opposite-cities-one-similar-battle-for-mayor/)).

In **District 3**, where Joy Lyndes is retiring, **Arielle Golden** (MBA, community organizer from Cardiff-by-the-Sea) favors workforce housing and a cooperative rather than confrontational approach to state law. She explicitly opposes downgrading the safety rating of bike lanes, particularly the one by the high school, **"where riders would move from a protected lane to between moving and parked cars"** ([Encinitas Action](https://encinitasaction.org/2026/08/26/meet-the-city-council-candidates-district-3/)). Her evolution is notable: she reportedly supported Ehlers, Shaffer, and O'Hara in 2024 but now runs on the Democratic ticket ([iNewsource](https://inewsource.org/2026/10/07/encinitas-california-city-council-mayor-2026-election/)). **Ed Sprague**, a retired deputy fire chief, highlights "community character, local control, public safety, and infrastructure investment" and pushed back against urbanization at forums. He did not respond to the progressive Encinitas Action questionnaire and received a Reform California endorsement ([North Coast Current](https://www.northcoastcurrent.com/election-2026/2026/07/ballot-starts-taking-shape-for-encinitas-city-council-election/); [KPBS](https://www.kpbs.org/news/politics/2026/09/30/2026-general-election-guide-to-endorsements-from-san-diego-democrats-republicans-green-libertarian)).

In **District 4**, **Brad Lefkowits** (Urban Forest Advisory Committee chair, sustainable landscape designer) is the most policy-detailed candidate. He wants Encinitas to explore **AB 787** to convert existing market-rate housing into deed-restricted affordable units, opposes spending taxpayer money fighting state housing laws **"without a realistic path to success,"** and cites four traffic fatalities in District 4 over three years as motivation for safer streets ([Encinitas Action](https://encinitasaction.org/2026/08/19/meet-the-city-council-candidates-district-4/)). However, he opposed a 100% affordable housing project on the L-7 property, calling it "a bridge too far" for the neighborhood. **Marco San Antonio**, a small business owner appointed by the Ehlers majority in January 2024, runs on defending Proposition A and **"standing firm against aggressive state-mandated housing laws"** ([North Coast Current](https://www.northcoastcurrent.com/encinitas-news/2026/10/then-and-now-encinitas-council-hopefuls-reflect-on-citys-40-years/)).

The forum consensus tells the real story of where Encinitas stands politically. **All six candidates** supported requiring two parking spaces per unit for new housing, opposed CEQA waivers, and backed red-light cameras and e-bike enforcement fines for parents ([iNewsource](https://inewsource.org/group-chat/encinitas-election-politics-housing/)). No candidate challenged parking minimums. The split was between "housing first" versus "treatment first" on homelessness (Golden and Lefkowits vs. everyone else) and between cooperation and confrontation with the state on housing mandates.

## Two-year terms and an Escondido mix-up explain the structural quirks

The "short term" label on the mayor's race reflects Encinitas's permanent institutional design, not a vacancy. When voters approved direct mayoral elections in 2014, they simultaneously rejected **Proposition M**, which would have set a four-year mayoral term. Every Encinitas mayoral election is therefore for a **two-year term** ([North Coast Current](https://www.northcoastcurrent.com/?p=1619); [Encinitas Municipal Elections](https://encinitasca.gov/Government/Municipal-Elections)). The 2026 winner will serve through November 2028. No resignation, recall, or mid-term departure produced the race; it is a standard re-election cycle. This structure creates unusually fast political oscillation: Kranz won the mayoralty in 2022, Ehlers replaced him in 2024, and Viskanta may replace Ehlers in 2026. The biennial accountability cycle means Encinitas evaluates its mayor twice as often as its council members, amplifying political volatility.

The two-year cycle also created a downstream cascade. When Ehlers moved from the District 4 council seat to mayor in 2024, the council appointed **Marco San Antonio** to fill the vacancy in January 2024 ([Encinitas Municipal Elections](https://encinitasca.gov/Government/Municipal-Elections)). An identical pattern occurred in 2022: when Kranz moved from the District 1 seat to mayor, the council appointed Allison Blackwell to fill his seat. Blackwell then lost to Luke Shaffer in 2024 by 13 points. San Antonio, the second consecutive appointed incumbent, is now losing to Lefkowits by 15 points. Appointed incumbents in Encinitas are 0-for-2 when they face voters.

The **District 4 vote count anomaly** initially flagged in the research (roughly 16,000 total votes in a race that should produce around 5,000) was entirely a data mix-up. The high-vote-total race was the **Escondido mayoral race** between Dane White and Elias Velazquez (combined 32,826 votes in a city of 151,000 residents), not an Encinitas district race at all. Escondido's figures appeared adjacent to Encinitas races in combined county results tables, causing the confusion. Actual Encinitas District 4 totals (5,547 votes) are consistent with the other district races (District 3: 5,026 votes) and roughly one-quarter of the citywide mayoral total (20,673 votes), exactly as expected for one of four districts ([Times of San Diego](https://timesofsandiego.com/community/2026/11/03/election-results-nov-3-2026-general-election/); [North Coast Current](https://www.northcoastcurrent.com/election-2026/2026/11/encinitas-mayors-race-tightest-such-contest-in-county/)).

## Endorsement lines harden into partisan trenches without YIMBY participation

The endorsement map in 2026 Encinitas is unusually clean for nominally nonpartisan city council races. The **San Diego County Democratic Party** endorsed all three challengers: Viskanta (mayor), Golden (D3), Lefkowits (D4). **U.S. Rep. Mike Levin** and **Assemblymember Tasha Boerner** endorsed Viskanta. **Sen. Blakespear backed the full challenger slate early** ([KPBS](https://www.kpbs.org/news/politics/2026/09/30/2026-general-election-guide-to-endorsements-from-san-diego-democrats-republicans-green-libertarian); [California Globe](https://californiaglobe.com/fr/from-the-cheap-seats-judgment-in-encinitas/)). On the other side, **Carl DeMaio's Reform California** endorsed Sprague and San Antonio; the **Deputy Sheriffs' Association** endorsed all three Ehlers-aligned candidates ([KPBS](https://www.kpbs.org/news/politics/2026/09/30/2026-general-election-guide-to-endorsements-from-san-diego-democrats-republicans-green-libertarian); [DSASD](https://dsasd.org/endorsements/)).

Two absences are revealing. The **San Diego County Republican Party endorsed in zero Encinitas races**, and the **Lincoln Club** likewise sat out, even though both endorsed heavily in Carlsbad and Oceanside. Reform California has effectively replaced the county GOP as the endorsing vehicle for conservative local candidates in coastal North County ([KPBS](https://www.kpbs.org/news/politics/2026/09/30/2026-general-election-guide-to-endorsements-from-san-diego-democrats-republicans-green-libertarian)). More striking is the absence on the progressive side: **YIMBY Action's November 2026 California slate includes 112 endorsements with zero in San Diego County**, and California YIMBY similarly skipped Encinitas ([YIMBY Action](https://yimbyaction.org/endorsements/november-2026-california-general)). The San Diego County Bicycle Coalition posted a letter supporting a Coastal Act challenge to the Santa Fe Drive removal but endorsed no candidates ([SD Bike Coalition](https://sdbikecoalition.org/blog/mwl2rljnc2qvn6xph6h14a6fp1jeh6)). The YIMBY movement has no footprint in Encinitas, because none of the Democratic-endorsed candidates ran on platforms that warrant it.

Viskanta's **nearly 2:1 fundraising advantage** ($72,226 vs. Ehlers' $42,307 as of late September) reflects the Democratic donor network's investment in flipping the mayoralty. With Encinitas's $500 per-donor cap, this advantage was built on breadth of support rather than a few large checks ([KPBS](https://www.kpbs.org/news/politics/2026/10/12/meet-the-candidates-running-for-encinitas-mayor-2026-general-election)). Blakespear's comfortable **SD-38 Senate lead (55.9% to 44.1%)** contrasts sharply with the razor-thin mayoral race ([NC Pipeline](https://ncpipeline.substack.com/p/2026-election-results-tight-races)). Voters are comfortable with Blakespear in Sacramento but divided on applying her policy approach locally. Viskanta's strategic necessity was to harness Blakespear's donor and organizational network while distancing herself from Blakespear's specific interventions, particularly the state bike lane legislation that even Viskanta opposed.

## Conclusion

The 2026 Encinitas results, if the preliminary leads hold, confirm that the bikelash wave peaked in 2024 but do not signal a pro-urbanist reversal. The political arc from Blakespear (progressive, state-aligned, pro-infrastructure) to Ehlers (confrontational, anti-mandate, anti-bike) to Viskanta (moderate, cooperative, pragmatic) traces a path toward an equilibrium position: voters rejected both the aggressive state-aligned approach and the scorched-earth response. The Ehlers council's inability to deliver on its core promises (Our Neighborhood Voices failed to qualify; the state blocked the bike lane removal; housing was approved under threat of decertification) likely mattered more than any ideological shift. A council that promised defiance but delivered compliance left its base without results and its opponents motivated.

The 2027 housing element planning cycle is the real test. If Viskanta, Golden, and Lefkowits take office with a 3-2 majority, they will inherit the 7th-cycle RHNA process (likely requiring significant new upzoning that could trigger Prop A's voter-approval requirement) with Shaffer and O'Hara holding seats through 2028. A slim majority with two hostile members and no YIMBY constituency to draw on means the new council will likely pursue the same pattern of grudging compliance with softer rhetoric. The most consequential outcome may be the simplest: a council that defers to staff on traffic engineering rather than designing bike lane removals from the dais, and one that works with HCD rather than suing. In Encinitas, that counts as progress.
