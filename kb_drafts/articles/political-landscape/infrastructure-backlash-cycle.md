# Infrastructure Backlash Cycle: How Anti-Urbanist Waves Rise and Break

## Overview

Active transportation and housing mandates generate local political backlash on a recognizable cycle: a grievance accumulation phase, an electoral wave, a governing phase that fails to deliver on its promises, and a correction. Understanding the cycle matters because advocates routinely misread each phase — treating the wave as a referendum on bike lanes or density when it is usually a referendum on implementation quality, and treating the correction as a mandate for urbanism when it is usually a mandate for procedural competence.

**Evidence base caveat.** This article is built primarily on one deeply documented case (Encinitas, California, 2018-2026). The phase model below is a hypothesis grounded in that case, not a validated cross-jurisdictional finding. Treat the Encinitas specifics as established and the generalizations as provisional until a second and third case are documented here.

---

## The Four-Phase Cycle

| Phase | Duration | Mechanism | Advocate error |
|-------|----------|-----------|----------------|
| **1. Grievance accumulation** | 2-6 years | Construction disruption, botched implementation, visible cost, opportunity-cost framing against unglamorous infrastructure | Dismiss complaints as NIMBYism; defend the mode instead of fixing the build |
| **2. Electoral wave** | 1 cycle | Single-issue opposition candidates consolidate diffuse grievance into a slate; incumbents lose on "stop development" / "stop the bike lanes" | Read the result as ideological rejection of urbanism |
| **3. Governing failure** | 1-2 years | Anti-mandate majority discovers it lacks legal authority; delivers rhetoric and reversal costs, not policy wins; base goes unsatisfied | Over-invest in fighting reversals that state law already blocks |
| **4. Correction** | 1 cycle | Moderates win on competence and tone, not on urbanism; policy substance barely moves | Claim the correction as a pro-housing or pro-bike mandate |

## Phase 1: Grievance Accumulation (Encinitas, 2018-2024)

Two separate projects, not one, built the Encinitas backlash. This matters: single-project analysis understates the accumulation.

**Leucadia Streetscape** was the slow-burn grievance. A 2.2-mile Highway 101 corridor project adding roundabouts, lane reductions, bike lanes, and sidewalks, discussed as early as 1995 and approved in March 2018. It produced years of construction disruption, with businesses complaining it drove away customers. Critics framed it as **over $20 million spent on surface upgrades while underground drainage in Leucadia remained underfunded** — an opportunity-cost argument against invisible infrastructure that is difficult to rebut and politically potent. By April 2020, more than **100 residents protested outside then-Mayor Catherine Blakespear's home**.

**Santa Fe Drive** was the acute flashpoint, and the failure was execution, not design intent. Funded by **$3.09 million in state money** secured by Sen. Blakespear in July 2023, the $4.15 million project installed Class IV protected bike lanes, reverse-angle parking, and lane narrowing. Then:

- A combined bike lane/sidewalk left inches between the sidewalk and the travel lane in places
- The **U.S. Postal Service stopped delivering mail for months**
- The city **fired the contractor in December 2024** over shoddy work
- Residents cited fire and ambulance access problems and seven blind right-hook turns
- A commission member presented data claiming a **more than 400% increase in serious crashes** versus the preceding 14 years

The crash claim is the kind of assertion that collapses under scrutiny — no exposure denominator, small case counts, and a construction-period confound. See [crash-data-methodology](../transportation-safety/crash-data-methodology.md). But it circulated as fact because the rest of the project's failures were real and verifiable. **Legitimate implementation grievances launder illegitimate safety claims.** That is the central lesson of Phase 1.

The state funding also created a direct link between a sitting state senator's Sacramento role and a local controversy, making the dispute personal and intergovernmental before it was ever electoral.

## Phase 2: The Electoral Wave (2024)

The wave is frequently misdated, including in advocacy retrospectives. In Encinitas it came in **2024**, not 2022.

**2022** (no wave): Blakespear departed for state Senate. Tony Kranz won the mayoral race with **48.18% in a four-candidate field**. Bruce Ehlers won the District 4 council seat; Joy Lyndes won District 3. The council remained progressive-leaning.

**2024** (the wave): Ehlers beat Kranz **52.4% to 47.6%**. Luke Shaffer won District 1 by 13 points (56.4%). Jim O'Hara won District 2 by 11 points (55.7%). Result: a **4-1 anti-mandate supermajority** with Lyndes as the lone dissenting voice. Kranz later attributed his loss primarily to Ehlers' promise to "stop development."

**Diagnostic for dating a wave correctly:** look for simultaneous multi-seat flips with margins outside the historical range, not for the first election in which an opposition figure wins any seat. Mixed-result cycles are noise; synchronized cycles are waves.

## Phase 3: Governing Failure (2025-2026)

The anti-mandate majority pursued a multi-front agenda and lost on every front that required legal authority it did not possess.

**Bike infrastructure.** In February 2026 the council voted to remove the protected bike lane in front of San Dieguito Academy High School. In March 2026 it voted **4-1** (Lyndes dissenting) to redesign the entire Santa Fe corridor from Class IV protected lanes to Class II painted lanes, replace reverse-angle parking with parallel spaces, and widen lanes to 11 feet — at an estimated **$3.4 to $3.6 million to undo a project that cost $4.15 million to build**. Ehlers argued protected lanes "are not necessarily safer than painted lanes" and that buffered lanes let cars pull over for emergency vehicles.

The state blocked it. Sen. Blakespear's SB 569, a statewide bike infrastructure protection bill, died in the Assembly. She then secured **budget trailer bill language specifically barring Encinitas from removing or modifying the Santa Fe Drive protected lane for five years**. The council voted 4-1 in September 2026 to ask the Governor to line-item veto the provision, with Ehlers and Deputy Mayor O'Hara calling it a "personal vendetta."

**Housing.** The council endorsed the **Our Neighborhood Voices** constitutional amendment, which would let local governments override conflicting state housing law. It failed to qualify for the 2022, 2024, and 2026 ballots. Blakespear said she saw **"no appetite in Sacramento"** for what Encinitas wanted; Assemblymember Chris Ward warned courts would uphold state law.

**And then it complied.** In February 2025 the council voted **3-1** to approve the **448-unit Quail Meadows project** (90 affordable units) after HCD warned that rejection risked housing element decertification and Builder's Remedy exposure. Ehlers called it a **"Sophie's choice."** The civic group Encinitas Action states the council approved every housing development that came before it. See [ca-housing-enforcement](../ca-housing-law/ca-housing-enforcement.md) for the compliance-under-threat pattern.

**The generalizable finding: an anti-mandate council changes the volume of the rhetoric, not the direction of the policy outcomes.** Prior Encinitas councils also opposed state mandates rhetorically and complied when penalties loomed. What the wave bought its voters was confrontation, reversal costs, and intergovernmental conflict — not fewer approved units.

This is also what breaks the wave. A council that promises defiance and delivers compliance leaves its own base without results while keeping its opponents mobilized.

## Phase 4: Correction Without Conversion (2026)

**Status: preliminary. Encinitas certifies on December 3, 2026. Numbers below are pre-certification counts and must be re-verified before citation.**

As of early counting: challenger Rimga Viskanta led Mayor Ehlers by **67 votes** (50.18% to 49.82%); Arielle Golden led Ed Sprague by **109 votes** in District 3 (50.96% to 49.04%); Brad Lefkowits led appointed incumbent Marco San Antonio by **856 votes** in District 4 (57.24% to 42.76%). If the leads hold, the council goes from a 4-1 anti-mandate majority to a 3-2 moderate majority in one cycle.

**The correction is not a conversion.** The critical evidence:

- **All six candidates** supported requiring two parking spaces per unit for new housing, opposed CEQA waivers for housing, and backed red-light cameras and e-bike enforcement fines for parents. No candidate challenged parking minimums. (On the enforcement question, see [automated-enforcement-evidence](../transportation-safety/automated-enforcement-evidence.md).)
- **YIMBY Action's November 2026 California slate carried 112 endorsements and zero in San Diego County.** California YIMBY also skipped Encinitas. The San Diego County Bicycle Coalition endorsed no candidates in the city.
- The winning candidates ran on **process, not mode**: deferring to staff on traffic engineering instead of "designing traffic projects from the dais," and working with legislators instead of litigating against the state. Viskanta opposed the state bike lane legislation that protected the project she wanted kept.
- Viskanta's position on housing is explicitly supply-skeptical: the city "cannot count on large-scale private developers to solve the affordable housing crisis," favoring nonprofit partnership, mobile home park preservation, and a strengthened inclusionary ordinance.

**The Overton window moved away from combat, not toward density.** Advocates who claim such a correction as a pro-housing mandate will overreach and seed the next wave.

## Structural Amplifiers

Local institutional design governs how fast the cycle runs. In Encinitas:

- **Two-year mayoral terms.** When voters approved direct mayoral elections in 2014 they simultaneously rejected Proposition M, which would have set a four-year term. Every Encinitas mayoral election is therefore for two years. The mayor faces voters twice as often as council members, roughly doubling oscillation speed: Kranz 2022, Ehlers 2024, possibly Viskanta 2026.
- **Appointment cascades.** A council member winning the mayoralty vacates a district seat, which the sitting majority fills by appointment. **Appointed incumbents in Encinitas are 0-for-2 when they face voters**: Allison Blackwell (appointed to Kranz's District 1 seat) lost to Shaffer by 13 points in 2024; San Antonio (appointed to Ehlers' District 4 seat) trails by roughly 15 points. Appointment by a faction is an electoral liability, not an incumbency advantage.
- **Voter-approval zoning requirements.** Encinitas Proposition A (2013), authored by Ehlers, requires voter approval for any zoning density or height increase. This converts every upzoning into a ballot campaign and will collide directly with 7th-cycle RHNA obligations. (Warrants its own article; see Open Questions.)

## Implications for Housing and Transportation Advocacy

1. **Build quality is political infrastructure.** The Santa Fe Drive design intent was sound and the execution handed opponents a multi-year campaign. Mail service interruption, emergency access complaints, and a fired contractor are not framing problems; they are construction defects with electoral consequences. Advocates should treat construction management, business mitigation, and phasing as core political work rather than as engineering detail someone else handles.
2. **Rebut the inflated safety statistic, but fix the real grievance first.** A "400% crash increase" claim fails on methodology, and saying so is useless while the sidewalk is inches from traffic. Credibility on the statistic depends on having addressed the verifiable failure.
3. **Do not fight reversals that state law already blocks.** The Santa Fe reversal was stopped by budget trailer bill language, not by local advocacy. Identify the state-law backstop early and spend local capital where no backstop exists.
4. **Expect compliance from anti-mandate councils, and document it.** Encinitas approved every housing project that came before an openly anti-mandate majority. This record is the single most useful rebuttal to the next "stop development" campaign, and it is also the wedge that separates such a majority from its base.
5. **Let the opposition's non-delivery do the work.** Our Neighborhood Voices failed to qualify three cycles running. "They promised to stop it and nothing stopped" beats any argument about density.
6. **A correction is not a constituency.** Where no YIMBY or bike coalition endorsed anyone, the incoming majority owes nothing to either. Expect grudging compliance with softer rhetoric, and build the constituency before claiming it.
7. **Audit local structural amplifiers before planning a multi-cycle campaign.** Term length, appointment practice, and voter-approval zoning requirements determine whether a win is durable or expires in 24 months.

## Open Questions

- **Second and third cases needed.** The phase model rests on one city. Candidate comparison cases in coastal North County (Carlsbad, Oceanside, Solana Beach) and the broader post-2020 bike lane removal wave should be documented before the model is treated as settled.
- **Voter-approval zoning charter measures** (Prop A and analogues) are not covered anywhere in this knowledge base and are a distinct obstruction mechanism from coastal density caps or RHNA rezoning delay. Warrants a dedicated article under `obstruction-patterns`.
- **Does the cycle require a state-funded project?** The intergovernmental personalization in Encinitas may be a necessary ingredient or merely an accelerant.
- **Appointed-incumbent penalty**: 0-for-2 in one city is suggestive, not evidence. Worth testing against a larger sample of California council appointments.

Carry these forward; do not cite the affected items until resolved.
